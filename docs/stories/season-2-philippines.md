## Rules for every Season 2 story

- **Ownership:** folklore and widely reported legends, retold in our own words. No specific author's creepypasta, Reddit post, book, komiks or film is copied. That includes *Hiwaga sa Balete Drive* (1988), the *Trese* comics and the Kampanerang Kuba komiks.
- **Real places and tragedies:**
  - Disputed claims are told as legend ("people say", "the story goes").
  - Victims, bodies and gore are never shown, and real dead are never mocked.
  - No living people and no suspects are named.
  - We don't tell viewers to trespass. Several sites are private property or businesses.
- **Folk beliefs are frightening, never stupid.** They are told the way a lola tells them: as rules that kept people alive. The camera takes them seriously, and so does the narrator. No "superstitious villagers" framing, no exaggerated accents, no comic duwende.
- **No stigma on real towns.** The manananggal and aswang stories are set in "a barrio in the Visayas", never in a named town or province (Capiz has long carried an unfair aswang stigma).
- **Facts here come from web-search summaries** (Inquirer, Philstar, GMA, ABS-CBN, Rappler, The Aswang Project and Wikipedia-derived pages, read through search results; full pages and primary books such as Maximo Ramos's *Creatures of Philippine Lower Mythology* were not read). ## 11. The Passenger on Balete Drive (reported legend)

**As people tell it:** a taxi driver on Balete Drive after midnight sees a woman in white flagging him down near a balete tree. In some versions she gets in, and when he looks in the rear-view mirror, the seat is empty, or her face is wrong. Some say she died on that road. Some say she has been there since the war.

**What is documented:**
- Balete Drive is a real two-lane street in New Manila, Quezon City, named after a large balete tree that once stood in the road.
- The White Lady legend was already in the press in the 1950s (a later Inquirer column recalls a 1950s newspaper investigation).
- The origin stories conflict: a girl killed on the road, a student, a woman from the Japanese occupation. **No real person has ever been identified.**
- The street is now lined with houses and condominiums.
- A 2005 proposal by a barangay official to declare it "haunted" for tourism was reported.

**Sources (search summaries):** Inquirer opinion ("'Real white lady' unmasked"), ABS-CBN News opinion (2021, "From ghost story to urban legend"), Wikipedia-derived pages on Balete Drive.

**Hook (frame 0):** the rear-view mirror at night. The back seat is empty; a rosary swings. "If you drive Balete Drive after midnight, don't look at the mirror."
Taglish: "Kung dadaan ka sa Balete Drive pagkalipas ng hatinggabi… huwag kang titingin sa salamin."

**Beats (pictures first):**
1. Mirror close-up; the rosary swings; the meter glows red.
2. A wide from the back seat: the driver's shoulders, the windshield, the street lights of New Manila.
3. The headlights find the balete: its hanging roots, wider than the road. A woman in white at its foot, her hand raised, her face hidden by her hair.
4. The driver doesn't stop. A side-window pass: she is closer than she should be.
5. Back in the cab: **the meter starts by itself**. The rosary stops swinging, as if held.
6. Mirror: empty. The driver glances back to the road. Mirror again: **black hair over the back-seat headrest**.
7. **Held silence.** Slow push into the mirror. The dashboard light rises on **half a pale face**, its eye open, the rest behind hair. Stinger in silence.
8. The driver turns: the back seat is empty. A ring of wet on the vinyl; **sampaguita petals**.
9. Through the windshield, far ahead: the balete again, the woman standing under it. Loop into the mirror.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Kung dadaan ka sa Balete Drive pagkalipas ng hatinggabi… huwag kang titingin sa salamin.
2. Ipinangalan ang kalyeng ito sa isang puno ng balete na dating nakatayo sa gitna ng daan.
3. Mula pa noong dekada singkwenta, iisa ang kuwento ng mga driver.
4. Isang babaeng nakaputi, sa tabi ng puno… pumapara.
5. May mga humihinto. Karamihan, hindi.
6. Hindi na mahalaga. Pagtingin nila sa salamin…
7. …may nakaupo na sa likod.
8. Sabi ng iba, dito siya namatay. Sabi ng iba, nandito na siya mula pa noong giyera. Walang nakaalam kung sino talaga siya.
9. Wala na ang puno. Bago na ang mga bahay.
10. Pero hanggang ngayon… umiikot pa rin ang mga driver.

**Narration (English draft, 10 lines; the source of the Taglish):**
1. If you drive Balete Drive after midnight, don't look at the mirror.
2. The street is named after a balete tree that once stood in the middle of the road.
3. Since at least the nineteen fifties, drivers have told the same story.
4. A woman in white, by the tree, waving down a ride.
5. Some stop. Most don't.
6. It doesn't matter. Later, they look in the mirror…
7. …and someone is sitting in the back.
8. Some say she died on this road. Some say she has been here since the war. No one has ever found out who she was.
9. The tree is gone now. The houses are new.
10. But drivers still take the long way around.

**Hidden details:**
1. Sampaguita petals already on the back seat in frame 0.
2. The meter reads the same fare at the start and at the end.
3. The balete appears in the mirror long after it has been passed.
4. In the side-window pass, her reflection is in the glass but not on the road.
5. The rosary hangs still, pulled towards the back seat, while she is there.

**Open question:** who was she, before she was a story?

**Sensitivity:**
- One popular version makes a taxi driver her killer. We drop that: it slanders a real trade, and no case exists.
- No violence and no named victim.
- The street is residential: no "go there tonight" call.

## 12. Tik-Tik (original, built on folklore)

**The belief (folklore, not our invention):** the *tiktik* is an aswang-type creature named after the sound it makes at night. **The fainter the sound, the closer it is; when it sounds loud, it is far away.** This is widely recorded in Tagalog and Visayan lore. Households protect themselves with salt, garlic and a light kept burning, and dogs bark at what people can't see.

**Our story (fiction):** a fiesta night in a barrio. The karaoke plays down the road. A lola tells her grandchild the rule. The sound comes: loud, then softer, then very soft. The karaoke stops mid-song. The dogs stop. The sound is inside the roof.

**Sources (search summaries):** folklore pages on the aswang and tiktik (The Aswang Project; Wikipedia-derived pages, citing Ramos).

**Hook (frame 0):** black, one dim bulb, a faint "tik… tik…". "If it's getting quieter, it isn't leaving. It's getting closer."
Taglish: "Pag humihina ang tunog… hindi siya lumalayo. Lumalapit siya."

**Beats:**
1. A dim bulb in a wooden house, a jalousie window, a banig on the floor, a child awake.
2. Exterior: a barrio street during the fiesta, banderitas strung overhead, the karaoke's coloured light far off, a sari-sari store with its shutter half down.
3. Lola, seen only as hands, ties garlic at the window and pours a line of salt across the sill.
4. Sound: "TIK-TIK" loud. Lola: "Malayo pa." (Still far.)
5. Softer. The karaoke song stutters; dogs bark all at once.
6. Very soft. **The karaoke stops mid-word. The dogs stop. The crickets stop.** Held silence.
7. The camera tilts up to the ceiling's sawali panels. Between two slats, **a long, wet tongue** lowers towards the sleeping child, slowly. (It is folk imagery; no wound is shown.)
8. The bulb flickers. In the flicker, **the face in the gap between roof and wall**: pale, eyes reflecting, a too-wide smile. Stinger in silence.
9. Black. A rooster crows far too early. The salt line is broken. Loop: the faint "tik… tik…" again.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Pag humihina ang tunog, hindi siya lumalayo. Lumalapit siya.
2. Sabi ng lola ko, nangangaso raw siya tuwing pista, habang malakas ang videoke at walang nakakarinig sa mga aso.
3. Nagtali siya ng bawang sa bintana. Nagbuhos siya ng asin sa pasimano.
4. Tapos narinig namin. Tik-tik. Malakas. "Malayo pa," sabi niya.
5. Tik… tik. Mahina na.
6. tik.
7. Tumigil ang videoke, sa gitna ng kanta.
8. Hindi gumalaw si Lola. Nakatingin siya sa bubong.
9. Kinaumagahan, nakakalat ang asin sa pasimano.
10. Mula sa loob.

The reveal moves to after line 8 (Lola looking at the roof, then what she sees). The child stays asleep and safe at dawn: the scattered salt says something was inside, not that anyone was harmed.

**Narration (English draft, the source of the Taglish):**
1. If it's getting quieter, it isn't leaving. It's getting closer.
2. My lola said it hunts on fiesta nights, when the music is loud and nobody hears the dogs.
3. She tied garlic to the window. She poured salt on the sill.
4. Then we heard it. Tik-tik. Loud. "Malayo pa," she said. Still far.
5. Tik… tik. Softer.
6. tik.
7. The karaoke stopped in the middle of the song.
8. Lola didn't move. She was looking at the roof.
9. In the morning, the salt on the sill was scattered.
10. From the inside.

**Hidden details:**
1. The karaoke screen shows the same song number at the start and the end.
2. A neighbour's window is open and dark in the street wide.
3. A shadow on the roof in the street wide, long before the attack.
4. The salt line has a gap from frame 0.
5. Lola's rosary beads are counted, and one is missing at the end.

**Open question:** who opened the window?

**Sensitivity:** an original, labelled fiction. No town is named. Lola is wise and right, not a figure of fun.

## 13. Half of Her (folklore: the manananggal)

**The belief:**
- The manananggal is a woman by day, often a quiet neighbour.
- At night she separates at the waist, grows bat-like wings and flies, leaving her lower half standing.
- She hunts the sleeping, especially pregnant women, with a long, thin tongue through gaps in the roof.
- The defence is to find the standing lower half and cover the open waist with **salt, crushed garlic or ash**, so she cannot rejoin it before sunrise.

These are among the most widely recorded Visayan beliefs, noted by Spanish-era chroniclers in general terms.

**Sources (search summaries):** Wikipedia-derived pages on the aswang and manananggal (citing Ramos and others); The Aswang Project. Low-quality AI-written pages in the results were not used.

**Hook (frame 0):** moonlight in a banana grove, a pair of legs standing perfectly still, nothing above the waist. "If you find half a woman standing in the banana trees, don't scream. Find the salt."
Taglish: "Kapag may nakita kang kalahating babae na nakatayo sa saging… huwag kang sisigaw. Hanapin mo ang asin."

**Beats:**
1. The banana grove wide at night; the lower half standing; a hut on stilts beyond.
2. Inside the nipa hut: a gasera lamp; a pregnant woman asleep on a banig; her husband awake with a bolo across his knees.
3. Sound: big wings outside. On the capiz window, **the shadow of wings** crosses.
4. The gasera flame leans towards the roof, as if pulled.
5. Between the nipa thatch: a long tongue lowers, slowly.
6. The husband looks up. **Reveal:** the manananggal clinging to the eaves outside: the torso, the wing membranes, the hair, a pale face lit by the lamp through the capiz. One clear, held shot. Stinger in silence.
7. He runs out, down the bamboo ladder, into the grove, with a jar of salt. The lower half is still standing.
8. He pours. Behind him, a wing-beat, a scream in the dark, then silence.
9. Dawn: the legs fallen in the grass. The wife asleep, safe. Loop to the grove at night.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Kapag may nakita kang kalahating babae na nakatayo sa sagingan, huwag kang sisigaw. Hanapin mo ang asin.
2. Sa Visayas, sabi nila, may mga babaeng dalawa ang buhay.
3. Sa araw, kapitbahay. Mabait. Tahimik.
4. Sa gabi, iniiwan niya ang kalahati ng sarili niya.
5. Ang kalahati, nagkakapakpak, at naghahanap ng mga natutulog. Lalo na ng mga inang naghihintay ng anak.
6. Mauuna mong maririnig ang mga pakpak. Tapos, hihilig ang apoy ng gasera papunta sa bubong.
7. Huwag kang titingala.
8. Lumabas ka. Hanapin mo ang kalahating iniwan niya. Takpan mo ng asin, o abo, o bawang.
9. Kapag hindi siya nakabalik bago sumikat ang araw,
10. hindi na siya makakabalik kailanman.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. If you find half a woman standing in the banana trees, don't scream. Find the salt.
2. In the Visayas, they say some women live two lives.
3. By day, a neighbour. Kind. Quiet.
4. At night, she leaves half of herself behind.
5. The other half grows wings… and hunts for the sleeping. Especially mothers who are waiting for a child.
6. You'll hear the wings first. Then the lamp will lean towards the roof.
7. Don't look up.
8. Go outside. Find the half she left behind. Cover it with salt, or ash, or garlic.
9. If she can't come back to it before sunrise…
10. …she never comes back at all.

**Hidden details:**
1. The lower half is in the grove in frame 0.
2. A neighbour woman greets the house at dusk in the opening wide, wearing the same skirt as the standing half.
3. Garlic hangs at the door and is missing one bulb.
4. The capiz window has one pane broken from the start.
5. At dawn, there are wing prints in the dust on the roof.

**Open question:** which neighbour didn't come to Mass that Sunday?

**Sensitivity:**
- No named town, and no Capiz.
- No harm to the pregnant woman or the baby is shown or described; she stays safe.
- The manananggal's end is off-screen, as sound.

## 14. The Guests of Dominican Hill (reported legend, Baguio)

**As people tell it:** at the ruined Diplomat Hotel on Dominican Hill, caretakers and visitors report knocking at night, the sounds of a dinner (plates, cutlery and voices) in empty halls, and **headless figures**, some said to be priests and nuns, walking the corridors.

**What is documented:**
- The Dominicans built the building as a rest house in the 1910s (most sources give 1913–1915).
- During the Second World War, civilians took shelter there. Accounts of the Japanese occupation differ: a garrison, or a place where prisoners were held.
- It was a hotel from about 1973 to 1987, then abandoned.
- The hill is now a heritage park.
- **The beheadings of priests and nuns are not documented** in the sources found. They are legend, and they are told as legend.

**Sources (search summaries):** Inquirer Business ("Haunted structures"), Inquirer News ("Baguio's 'haunted hill' @100"), The Aswang Project, Atlas Obscura, Wikipedia-derived pages.

**Hook (frame 0):** a flashlight in a ruined hall at 3 a.m.: a long table set for dinner, with candles. "In Baguio, there is a hotel where the guests still come down to dinner."
Taglish: "Sa Baguio, may hotel na bumababa pa rin ang mga bisita para maghapunan."

**Beats:**
1. A flashlight POV, the table set for dinner, cutlery gleaming.
2. Exterior: pine trees in Baguio fog; the white, roofless building on the hill; the empty windows.
3. Wartime, told in picture only: candlelit families sheltering in a corridor (silhouettes, no harm shown).
4. A caretaker's round: the hallway, knocking from behind a door that has no room behind it.
5. From the dining hall, the sound of plates and low voices. The flashlight swings: empty.
6. The flashlight goes out. In the dark, the scrape of chairs.
7. **Reveal:** the light comes back. At the far end of the table, **a figure in a white habit, seated, with nothing above the collar**: clean, no wound, a silhouette that is simply missing. One held, well-lit shot. Stinger in silence.
8. A cut away and back: the chair is empty; **every chair is now pulled out**.
9. Exterior dawn: fog, the ruin. Loop to the table.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Sa Baguio, may hotel na bumababa pa rin ang mga bisita para maghapunan.
2. Itinayo ito ng mga Dominikano sa burol, mahigit isang daang taon na ang nakalipas.
3. Noong giyera, dito nagtago ang mga tao.
4. Kalaunan, naging hotel. Tapos, iniwan na lang sa hamog.
5. Sabi ng mga tagapagbantay, nagsisimula ang katok pagkalipas ng hatinggabi.
6. Tapos, tunog ng mga plato. At mga boses, sa silid-kainan.
7. May mga nakakita raw ng mga pigura sa pasilyo. Mga pari. Mga madre.
8. Wala ni isa sa kanila ang may ulo.
9. Walang tala ang mga historyador na nangyari talaga iyon.
10. Pero tanungin mo ang kahit sinong taga-Baguio. Huwag ka nang maghapunan doon.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. In Baguio, there is a hotel where the guests still come down to dinner.
2. It was built on Dominican Hill more than a century ago, as a rest house for priests.
3. In the war, people hid inside these walls.
4. Later, it was a hotel. Then it was left to the fog.
5. The caretakers say the knocking starts after midnight.
6. Then the sound of plates. And voices, in the dining hall.
7. People tell of figures in the corridors. Priests. Nuns.
8. None of them has a head.
9. Historians have found no record of what the stories claim happened here.
10. But ask anyone in Baguio, and they will tell you: don't stay for dinner.

**Hidden details:**
1. The table has one more place setting at the end than at the start.
2. A figure stands in an upper window in the exterior wide.
3. A candle is lit in the dawn shot, when no one is there.
4. The caretaker's logbook shows three knocks at 3:00 every night.

**Open question:** who set the table?

**Sensitivity:**
- Wartime suffering at the site is real. It is shown only as people sheltering, with no violence.
- The beheadings are clearly marked as legend.
- The headless figure has no wound: an absence, not gore.
- The site is a park; we don't encourage night entry.

## 15. Don't Pick Up the Baby (folklore: the tiyanak)

**The belief:**
- The tiyanak imitates a newborn's cry, alone in the forest or tall grass, to lure travellers. When it is picked up, it shows its true form: a small, ancient creature with claws and fangs.
- In the version from the Spanish period, tiyanak are the souls of infants who died before baptism.
- Variants differ by region: in a Mindoro version it becomes a black bird, and in a Pampanga version it is a small brown being that floats.
- Some say travellers lost to its crying can break the spell by wearing their clothes inside out.

**Sources (search summaries):** Wikipedia (Tiyanak, citing Eugenio's *Philippine Folk Literature* and Ramos), DZRH News. Embellished story pages were not used.

**Hook (frame 0):** headlights on cogon grass; a white bundle; a baby's cry. "On a dark road, if you hear a baby crying in the grass, keep driving."
Taglish: "Kapag may narinig kang umiiyak na sanggol sa damuhan… huwag kang hihinto."

**Beats:**
1. The last jeepney trip of the night ("LAST TRIP" on its signboard), an empty provincial road, the driver alone.
2. The cry from the grass. He slows; the jeep's interior lights flicker.
3. A bundle in a lampin at the roadside, white and still, crying.
4. He picks it up and carries it into the jeep, to the light.
5. The cry stops. The bundle is heavier. He adjusts his grip.
6. **Reveal:** the lampin falls open. **A wrinkled, ancient face with a baby's body**, eyes black, a mouth too full of teeth. Well lit by the jeep's fluorescent tube. Stinger in silence.
7. The jeep's lights go out.
8. Morning: the jeep parked off the road, its door open, the driver's tsinelas on the step, his shirt on the seat **turned inside out**.
9. Night again, the same road: the cry in the grass. Loop.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Sa madilim na daan, kapag may narinig kang umiiyak na sanggol sa damuhan, huwag kang hihinto.
2. Sa probinsya, tiyanak ang tawag dito.
3. Parang bagong silang. Mag-isa. Nilalamig. Umiiyak, naghihintay na may bumuhat.
4. Humihinto ang mga driver. Siyempre.
5. Magaan pa sa una. Tapos, bumibigat.
6. At tumitigil ang iyak.
7. Sabi ng iba, sila raw ang mga kaluluwa ng mga batang hindi nabinyagan. Sabi naman ng iba, mas matanda pa sila roon.
8. Sabi ng matatanda: kapag naligaw ka at narinig mo ito, baligtarin mo ang damit mo.
9. Nalilito raw sila.
10. May mga driver na nakaalala sa tamang oras.

**Narration (English draft):**

## 16. Ward Three (reported legend, Clark)

**As people tell it:** at the abandoned hospital of the former Clark Air Base in Angeles, Pampanga, visitors report apparitions, voices, shadows, screams, stones thrown by no one, and a woman in white seen crossing the road nearby (sometimes in the back seat of passing cars).

**What is documented:**
- Clark was a US base until 1991.
- The hospital building was abandoned.
- It was featured by Ghost Hunters International and National Geographic's *I Wouldn't Go In There*.
- Philstar reported a 2005–06 ghost-hunting craze at Clark, and officials who declined to comment.

**Not settled:** the sources differ on the hospital's history (some say the Second World War, some the Vietnam era). Everything paranormal is told as report.

**Sources (search summaries):** Philstar (2005, 2006, 2013), Inquirer Business ("Haunted haunts"), Rappler (ghost hunting, 7 places), Punto Central Luzon.

**Hook (frame 0):** a flashlight down a black corridor; a wheelchair at the end **slowly turning to face the camera**. "People say this hospital was abandoned. They say it was never empty."
Taglish: "Abandonado na raw ang ospital na 'to… pero hindi raw ito kailanman naging bakante."

**Beats:**
1. Corridor POV with a flashlight; the wheelchair turns.
2. Exterior: the long, low hospital in tall grass at night, the Pampanga sky orange from the city far off.
3. A group of ghost hunters (generic, faceless) with a camcorder at the entrance.
4. Inside: peeling paint, a ward sign "WARD 3", beds without mattresses.
5. A pebble clicks on the floor. Another. They come from behind.
6. The camcorder viewfinder (handheld): night-vision green; at the end of the ward, **a nurse in an old white uniform standing between the beds**, head bowed.
7. The camera looks away, at a friend, and back. She is **three beds closer**. Repeat. **Reveal:** she lifts her head in the viewfinder, face full, eyes white. Stinger in silence. The tape tears.
8. Exterior: the group running to the car. In the car, the back seat. **A white sleeve.**
9. Loop: the corridor, the wheelchair.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Abandonado na raw ang ospital na 'to. Pero hindi raw ito kailanman naging bakante.
2. Clark, Pampanga. Halos isang siglo, base militar ng mga dayuhan.
3. Nang magsara ang base, naiwan ang ospital.
4. Tapos, nagsimula ang mga kuwento. Mga boses sa mga palapag na walang tao. Mga sigaw sa damuhan.
5. Sabi ng mga bumibisita, may bumabato sa kanila. Mula sa dilim. Pero walang tao.
6. Dumating ang mga ghost hunter, may dalang camera. Pati mga taga-TV.
7. Marami raw ang nakakita ng nurse na nakaputi, sa pagitan ng mga kama.
8. Huwag mong aalisin ang tingin mo sa kanya.
9. Sa kalsada sa labas, sabi ng mga driver, may babaeng nakaputi na tumatawid sa harap ng sasakyan nila...
10. ...o nakaupo sa likod nila.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. People say this hospital was abandoned. They say it was never empty.
2. Clark, Pampanga. For almost a century, a foreign air base.
3. When the base closed, the hospital was left behind.
4. Then the stories started. Voices on empty floors. Screams in the grass.

**Open question:** what did they hear on the tape?

**Sensitivity:**
- Real people died at war-era and base hospitals; the nurse is a legend figure, not a real person.

## 17. The Road That Wouldn't End (folklore: tikbalang)

**The belief:**
- The tikbalang is a tall creature with a horse's head and very long limbs; when it squats, its knees rise above its head. It haunts mountain roads, forests and bamboo groves.
- It leads travellers astray, so they walk or drive in circles. When people get lost, they still say half-jokingly, "May tikbalang dito."
- The remedies are to **turn your shirt inside out** and to say **"tabi tabi po"** (excuse me) when passing its places.
- **Rain while the sun shines** is "a tikbalang's wedding" (*kasal ng tikbalang*).
- The kapre (a cigar-smoking giant in balete and acacia trees) shares these places.

**Sources (search summaries):** Philippine Daily Tribune ("Where spirits dwell", 2026), Inquirer ("Balete tree's eeriness…"), Wikipedia-derived pages on the tikbalang and kapre, the Sacred Texts copy of *Philippine Folklore Stories*.

**Hook (frame 0):** a tricycle's headlight passes a sari-sari store with a hand-painted sign, "TINDAHAN NI ALING NENA", for the third time. "If you pass the same store three times, stop the tricycle. Turn your shirt inside out."
Taglish: "Kung tatlong beses mo nang nadaanan ang parehong tindahan… huminto ka. Baligtarin mo ang damit mo."

**Beats:**
1. Tricycle POV at night: a barrio road, bamboo on both sides, the sari-sari store.
2. A wide: the tricycle's single light in a long dark road, a balete at the bend. In its branches, **an ember glows** (the kapre's cigar).
3. The rider passes the balete, the store, a chapel, a waiting shed. Then the store again.
4. He speeds up. The store, again; its bulb flickers.
5. Rain starts while the moon is out (the tikbalang's wedding, transposed to moonlight).
6. He stops. Silence. Bamboo creaks. Something laughs, deep and low, like a horse breathing.
7. **Reveal:** in the headlight, between the bamboo, **a figure squatting with its knees above its head**, a long horse face lowering into the light. Stinger in silence.
8. He pulls his shirt off, turns it inside out and whispers "tabi tabi po". Black.
9. Dawn: the road, the store, the tricycle parked. On the mud road, **hoofprints** circling the tricycle. Loop: the store at night.

**Narration (Taglish, for Juanito):**
1. Kapag tatlong beses mo nang nadaanan ang parehong tindahan, huminto ka. Baligtarin mo ang damit mo.
2. Sa mga lumang daan, may mahilig daw makipaglaro sa mga manlalakbay.
3. Matangkad. Mas matangkad pa sa kawayan. Ulo ng kabayo, katawan ng tao.
4. Hindi ka niya sinasaktan. Itinatago ka niya.
5. Tuloy ka sa pagmamaneho. Tuloy. At bumabalik ang parehong tindahan.
6. Sabi ng mga lola: kapag dumaan ka sa malalaking puno, magsabi ka ng "tabi tabi po."
7. Karamihan, sinasabi na lang nang hindi iniisip.
8. Ngayong gabi, nakalimutan niya.
9. Nang baligtarin niya sa wakas ang damit niya, pinakawalan siya ng daan.
10. Natagpuan ang tricycle niya kinaumagahan, dalawandaang metro mula sa bahay niya.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. If you pass the same store three times, stop. Turn your shirt inside out.
2. On the old roads, they say something likes to play with travellers.
3. Tall. Taller than the bamboo. A horse's head on the body of a man.
4. It doesn't hurt you. It keeps you.
5. You drive. And drive. And the same store comes back.
6. Lolas say: when you pass the big trees, say "tabi tabi po". Excuse me.
7. Most people say it without thinking.
8. Tonight, he forgot.
9. When he finally turned his shirt inside out, the road let him go.
10. They found his tricycle at dawn, two hundred metres from his house.

**Hidden details:**
1. The kapre's cigar ember in the balete in every pass.
2. The store's price list changes on each pass.
3. The rain falls while the moon is clear (the tikbalang's wedding).
4. Hoofprints circle the tricycle at dawn.
5. In the third pass, the figure is already standing behind the store, unlit.

**Open question:** did you say "tabi tabi po" on your way home?

**Sensitivity:** the beliefs are treated as wisdom that saves the rider. No comic tikbalang. The store's name is invented.

## 18. The Light in the Quezon Room (reported legend, Malacañang)

**As people tell it:** for generations, palace staff have told stories:
- lights switching on late at night in the Quezon Room in Kalayaan Hall;
- the pacing of a past president during times of crisis;
- a figure smoking in the Roxas Room;
- a child seen playing there;
- a headless priest in the hall (a 2016 employee account).

**What is documented:**
- Kalayaan Hall in the Malacañang complex was built in 1920–21 as the Executive Building. It now houses the Presidential Museum and Library.
- The stories themselves are **staff and family recollections** reported in the press (Arab News, Philstar Life, Manila Bulletin, Daily Tribune). They are anecdotes, not records.
- Do not confuse it with UP's Kalayaan Residence Hall, which has its own legend.

**Sources (search summaries):** Philstar Life ("Malacañang Palace ghosts"), Arab News ("In Philippine presidential palace, staffers share generations of haunted stories"), Manila Bulletin ("The haunted palace", 2023), Daily Tribune (2023).

**Hook (frame 0):** the palace at night, every window dark. One switches on. "At night, in the palace, one office light turns on by itself."
Taglish: "Gabi-gabi, sa Malacañang, may isang ilaw na kusang bumubukas."

**Beats:**
1. The palace by the Pasig at night; capiz windows; one lights up.
2. A guard's round: a long hall with wooden floors, portraits, a flashlight.
3. The door to the old office: a strip of light under it.
4. Inside: a desk lamp lit; a chair turned towards the window; an empty room.
5. Footsteps pacing above, on wooden boards, back and forth.
6. In the next room, cigarette smoke curls in the light, from no one.
7. A child's laugh at the end of the hall. A ball rolls out of a doorway.
8. **Reveal:** the guard turns. At the far end of the hall, a **figure in a cassock, with nothing above the collar**, walks away into the dark. Stinger in silence.
9. The window from outside: the light switches off. Loop.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. At night, in the palace, one office light turns on by itself.

8. And once, an employee said, a priest walking the corridor without a head.
9. The guards don't explain it. They just turn off the light.
10. It's on again the next night.

**Hidden details:**

3. A small shoe in the hall.
4. Smoke in the window from outside in the last shot.

**Open question:** what is he still worried about?

**Sensitivity:**

- The palace is not shown as anything but historic.

## 19. Santelmo (folklore and science)

**The belief:**
- The santelmo (from St. Elmo's fire) is a ball of fire seen at night over fields, swamps, rivers and the sea.
- It follows travellers and leads them into mud and thorns, and some say people who chase it lose their minds.
- Many say it is **the soul of someone who drowned**, or of someone never properly mourned or buried.
- The remedy is to turn your clothes inside out. A Mindoro tale tells of sailors fighting one off with vinegar, salt and water.
- Indigenous names for it are older than the Spanish term.

**What science says:** **St. Elmo's fire** is a real, documented glow (a corona discharge on masts and points in stormy air). Ball lightning is reported but poorly understood, and marsh gas has been proposed for lights over swamps. None explains a light that follows you.

**Sources (search summaries):** The Aswang Project (santelmo and santilmo), Wikipedia-derived pages on the Santelmo and St. Elmo's fire.

**Hook (frame 0):** dark rice paddies; a ball of fire above the palay, keeping exact pace with the camera. "If a light follows you across the rice fields, don't follow it back."
Taglish: "Kapag may liwanag na sumusunod sa'yo sa palayan… huwag mo itong sundan."

**Beats:**
1. The pilapil (the raised path between paddies), a farmer's flashlight, the santelmo keeping pace.
2. A wide: the paddies under clouds, a nipa hut, the canal, the irrigation pump; frogs.
3. The farmer stops; the light stops. He walks; it walks.
4. It drifts over the canal. Its reflection is in the water, **and under the water a second glow**.
5. The frogs stop.
6. It splits into three.
7. **Reveal:** the light comes close, at face height. Inside the fire, for four frames, **a drowned face**, mouth open, hair floating as if underwater. Stinger in silence.
8. He turns his shirt inside out; the light shrinks, drifts back over the canal and sinks into the water.
9. Dawn: a mark of scorched palay in a circle. Loop to the night paddies.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. If a light follows you across the rice fields, don't follow it back.
2. Farmers call it santelmo.
3. A ball of fire, floating over the paddies. Over the rivers. Over the sea.
4. Scientists know a real glow called St. Elmo's fire. It clings to masts in storms.
5. It doesn't follow you home.
6. The old people say the santelmo is a soul. Someone who drowned, and was never found.
7. It leads you to the water.
8. Because it wants company.
9. Turn your clothes inside out, they say. It loses you.
10. Most nights.

**Hidden details:**
1. The glow under the canal water is there from the first wide.
2. A pair of tsinelas on the pilapil, wet.
3. The scorched circle in the palay at dawn.
4. A fourth light far off in the last wide.

**Open question:** who never came home from the river?

**Sensitivity:** drowning is real in farming and fishing communities. No real drowning is depicted, and the face is a legend image, not a person.

## 20. The Theatre by the Bay (reported legend over a real accident)

**As people tell it:** the Manila Film Center is the most haunted building in Manila:
- workers killed in its rushed construction, some said to be **buried in the concrete**;
- voices, figures in the theatre and on the stairs, cold spots;
- performers and guards sensing people in the empty seats.

**What is documented:**
- The building was rushed for the 1982 Manila International Film Festival.
- **On 17 November 1981, at about 3 a.m., scaffolding collapsed** and workers fell into the setting cement.
- The festival opened on schedule.

**Disputed, and told as disputed:**
- **The number of dead is not settled:** a figure of seven was printed; rumours ran to 169. A 2005 documentary is reported to have traced the workers and found no more than about a dozen dead.
- How long rescuers were held back, and whether workers were entombed, is told in legend versions that the sources do not establish.

**Sources (search summaries):** Inquirer Opinion ("Account on the 1981 Manila Film Center deaths"), GMA Public Affairs ("Multo ng nakaraan"), Inquirer Business ("Haunted structures"), Daily Tribune (2026), SCMP (archive).

**Hook (frame 0):** a theatre at night, rows of folded red seats in a flashlight beam. One seat **folds down by itself**. "In this theatre, someone is always sitting in the empty seats."
Taglish: "Sa teatrong ito… laging may nakaupo sa mga bakanteng upuan."

**Beats:**
1. The flashlight on the seats; one folds down.
2. Exterior: the white building on the reclaimed land by Manila Bay, its columns like a temple, the bay black behind.
3. 1981, in picture only: scaffolding against the building at night, work lights; the empty scaffold sways. Cut to black at 3:00 on a site clock. **No people falling, nobody shown.**
4. Silence. The festival lights the next season: searchlights, empty red carpet (no faces).
5. The present: a guard's flashlight on the grand staircase.
6. Footsteps above. The sound of a trowel scraping concrete.
7. In the theatre, the seats: one, then several, fold down, in a row, as if people are taking their seats.
8. **Reveal:** the flashlight sweeps the rows. Every seat is down. **Rows of figures sitting in the dark, all facing the stage, all grey with dust.** Their faces are never shown, only their backs and the dust. Stinger in silence. One turns its head.
9. Lights out. A single hard hat on a seat, then gone. Loop to the folded seats.

**Narration (English draft):**
1. In this theatre, someone is always sitting in the empty seats.
2. Manila, nineteen eighty one. A film center is rushed to be finished in months.
3. At three in the morning, on November seventeenth, the scaffolding collapsed.
4. Workers fell into the wet cement.
5. How many died has never been settled. Seven, said one report. More, said the workers' stories.
6. The festival opened on time.
7. Guards say they hear hammering at night. And footsteps, on stairs no one is using.
8. Performers say the seats are full when the lights go down.
9. People call them ghosts.
10. Maybe they're just waiting for their names.

**Hidden details:**
1. A site clock at 3:00 in the 1981 shot, and a clock at 3:00 in the present.
2. One seat is already down in frame 0.
3. A hard hat on a seat in the dark wide.
4. Handprints in the dust on the stage edge.

**Open question:** how many were there?

**Sensitivity:**
- This is a real accident with real dead and grieving families. No worker is shown falling, trapped or dead.
- The figures are dust-grey backs, never faces, never wounds.
- The toll is stated as disputed; the entombment is legend.
- No living person is named. The government of the day is "the government of the time". No political point-scoring.
- The last line honours the workers rather than making them monsters.
