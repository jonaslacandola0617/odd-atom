## Ep 31 "Dalawa Sila" (There Are Two of Them): Clarita Villanueva, Manila, 1953

**The story.** In May 1953 a teenage girl held at the Manila City Jail (the old Bilibid) began screaming that two invisible beings were biting her: one huge and dark, one small. Guards, doctors and reporters said they saw bite marks, wet with saliva, appear on her arms, neck and shoulders. Mayor Arsenio Lacson came and later told the press he had seen it. An American Pentecostal preacher, Lester Sumrall, prayed over her for days; then the attacks stopped.

**Claimed vs documented.**
- *Documented:* the 1953 Manila press covered it (the *Daily Mirror* called it a "yarn" at first, per Sumrall's book), and a US wire story ran "Manila Mayor Confirms 'Spook' Attacks On Girl" (20 May 1953). City health officers and doctors examined her. The 2019 film *Clarita* is based on it.
- *Claimed:* the marks appearing in front of witnesses; the two beings; that prayer ended it. Much of the detail comes from Sumrall, a partisan source. Her age differs by source (15, 17 or 18). Some reports mention a medical diagnosis that was later set aside.

**Sources (all summaries):** [Wikipedia: Possession of Clarita Villanueva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possession_of_Clarita_Villanueva); [Philstar 2019, the film's director on his research](https://www.philstar.com/entertainment/2019/06/14/1926479/netflix); [Inquirer Opinion "The Thing"](https://opinion.inquirer.net/143757/the-thing/amp); [FilipiKnow true stories](https://www.filipiknow.net/true-stories-from-philippine-history); Sumrall, *Demons: The Answer Book* (excerpt via search).

**Scare beats.** A bite rises on clean skin under a flashlight (hook) → the cell at night, a tin cup rolls → a doctor's hand presses the mark: it's wet → the second, smaller bite beside it → the mayor's hat and polished shoes at the bars (no face) → the guard's lamp swings; on the wall, two shadows that belong to no one in the cell, one tall, one small → silence → a fresh bite on **the doctor's** wrist as he writes his report (our dramatisation, never claimed).

**Form: "the examination."** We only ever see **hands, arms and paper**: her forearm in a pool of torchlight, the examiners' hands, a typewriter writing the police report, the newspaper being typeset. The girl is never seen above the shoulder. The camera always drifts like a reporter's eye leaning in.
- **Scare placement:** the hook is the scare (frame 0); then a slow tightening; the big scare is the transfer at the end, in the last 3 s.
- **Sound language:** no score. The jail's ceiling fan, a typewriter, a flashbulb, a tin cup, her breath held (never her voice or screams), and wet skin. The bites are a soft, close, ugly *pressure* sound, not a crunch.
- **Visual language:** 1953 black-and-white newsprint halftone for the press cutaways; warm torchlight on skin in the cell; the halftone dots "move" as the press runs.

**Care.** She was a minor and a real person; we never show her face, never name her on screen beyond what the public record uses (the narration can say "a girl named Clarita"), never sexualise her, and say plainly that doctors had their own explanation. Lacson and Sumrall are historical public figures, shown only as hat, shoes and hands. No religion is mocked; the narration reports the prayer, it does not endorse or ridicule it.

## Ep 32 "Tatlong Katok" (Three Knocks): the kumakatok

**The story.** In Tagalog folklore, three hooded figures walk the streets at night and knock three times on a door. Within days, someone in that house dies: the eldest, or the one who is already sick. Opening or not opening the door makes no difference. People once painted white crosses on their doors; the lore says the three then moved on to hospitals and churches. Sightings were said to rise after epidemics.

**Claimed vs documented.** Folklore only. Most detailed modern sources trace to *The Balete Book* (Paraiso and Paraiso, 2003). Accounts differ on who the three are (one young woman and two old men, or three men). Nothing documented.

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Kumakatok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumakatok); [Aswang Project: Nangangatok](https://aswangproject.com/nangangatok-door-knocking-spirits); [The Line Up: Philippine ghostly legends](https://the-line-up.com/philippines-ghostly-legends).

**Scare beats.** Three knocks on a door across the street; that house's light comes on (hook) → a crane shot down the barangay: three robed figures pass under the one streetlight → house by house, knock-knock-knock; in each, a light comes on, and a candle is later lit in the window → a mother hurries to chalk a white cross on our door → the knocks come up our street, skipping the crossed doors → silence at our door; they pass → relief → three knocks **from inside**, on Lola's bedroom door.

**Form: "the street."** One barangay at midnight, told from **above and outside** (a slow crane over the roofs, then across from the houses through jalousie slats), never inside a room until the last shot. The pattern of lights is the story: each knock lights a window, each death is a candle.
- **Scare placement:** an early mini-scare (the hook), a long relief beat (they pass), then the twist at the end.
- **Sound language:** knocks only, always in threes; dogs that start barking and then stop, one by one, along the street; a tricycle far away; slippers on concrete. No score.
- **Visual language:** a sodium-orange street, white chalk crosses that glow faintly, black windows that light up yellow, then flicker to candle orange.

**Care.** Folklore told as folklore. No real barangay named. Lola's fate is never shown (the episode ends on the knock). No mockery of the white-cross custom.

## Ep 33 "Igalaw Mo ang Daliri" (Move Your Toe): the batibat and bangungot

**The story.** In Ilocano belief, the batibat is a huge, fat old female spirit who lives in a tree. If the tree is cut and made into a post of a house, she comes with it, and anyone who sleeps near her post wakes unable to move, with her weight on their chest. The cure people repeat: bite your thumb or **wiggle your big toe**, and you'll wake.

**Claimed vs documented.**
- *Documented:* bangungot is also the folk name for sudden unexplained nocturnal death. A review of Manila cases from 1948 to 1982 found 96% of victims were men, mean age 33, and the most common time of death **3 a.m.** Medical explanations include Brugada syndrome and sleep apnoea. Sleep paralysis is real and common.
- *Claimed:* the batibat herself; that she comes with the post.

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Batibat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batibat); [Aswang Project: Batibat / bangungot](https://aswangproject.com/batibat-bangungot); [Inquirer Opinion: The nightmare called 'bangungot'](https://opinion.inquirer.net/106787/nightmare-called-bangungot/amp); [Sleepopolis: the batibat](https://sleepopolis.com/?p=117293).

**Scare beats.** Black; breath; a weight presses the camera down (hook) → daytime: an old tree is cut, a trunk becomes a house post → the new house at night, a man asleep on a banig beside the post → the post's grain moves like skin → 2:59 on a wall clock → his POV: he can't move; the ceiling; a vast soft shape leaning over him → "Igalaw mo ang daliri…" → the shot cuts to his big toe, trying, trembling → it moves → he gasps awake → his wife, beside him, isn't breathing evenly… and her toe is still.

**Form: "paralysis POV."** Most of the episode is **the sleeper's own view**, locked to his head (so it moves only with his breathing and tiny eye darts), cut hard against the one thing he's trying to move: his toe, in extreme close-up. The daytime prologue (the tree) is the only wide shot.
- **Scare placement:** the hook, a long creeping middle with no release, a false relief, then the last image.
- **Sound language:** heartbeat and his own strained breath, close and dry; the post creaking like a living trunk; a rooster that crows far too early; when she leans in, everything goes muffled, as if heard from under water.
- **Visual language:** blue-black moonlight through capiz, the dark mass reading only as a silhouette against the moonlit wall, never a clear face; the toe lit by the same moonlight.

**Care.** Real people die of bangungot; no death is shown, and the narration gives the medical facts (and that a doctor should check anyone with symptoms). The wife's state is left ambiguous. The batibat is never made comic about her size.

## Ep 34 "Paatras" (Backwards): the sigbin

**The story.** In Visayan and Mindanao folklore, the sigbin is a creature that **walks backwards with its head lowered between its hind legs**, like a hornless goat or a dog with long hind legs and huge ears. It comes out at night, drinks blood, and is said to be kept by certain families, carried in clay jars. Its smell is foul.

**Claimed vs documented.** Folklore only. Descriptions vary (goat, dog, kangaroo-like). A web "sighting log" (1967 Tubigon, Bohol) looks invented and is not used. No province is named.

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Sigbin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigbin); [Philstar Freeman 2013: local cryptids](https://philstar.com/cebu-lifestyle/2013/10/28/1250370/halloween-special-local-cryptids); [Thus.ph: lesser-known creatures](https://thus.ph/articles/monsters-in-the-margins-the-lesser-known-creatures-of-philippine-folklore/).

**Scare beats.** Prints in the mud pointing away from the house, and something behind them coming closer (hook) → the farm at night; the goats in their pen won't lie down → a lamp; tracks again, going "out" of the pen, yet a goat is gone → the dog, tied, whimpers and backs into its kennel → the farmer follows the tracks the way they point, away from the house… → we cut back: the creature, reversing toward the house in the dark, ears dragging → it reaches the window where his child sleeps → he turns: the prints were leading to the house all along → the head between the legs looks up at us, upside down.

**Form: "the tracker."** Ground-level tracking shots that **follow footprints**, cut against the creature's own reversing movement. The audience learns the rule with the farmer (prints point the wrong way) and sees the danger before he does.
- **Scare placement:** a wrongness from frame 0, then dread by cross-cutting; the payoff (the inverted face) at the end.
- **Sound language:** wet mud, goats bleating then silent, a chained dog's whine, and the sigbin's steps played in an unnatural rhythm (heel-toe reversed); no score.
- **Visual language:** low kerosene light and a big moon on wet paddies; the creature mostly in silhouette against the lit window, the face only at the end.

**Care.** No family is shown or accused of keeping a sigbin (the "keeper families" belief has been used to stigmatise real people); the narration mentions the belief as folklore only. The child is safe in frame; the episode ends before anything happens to anyone.

## Ep 35 "Bantay" (The Watch): the amalanhig and the lamay

**The story.** Why do Filipinos stay awake all night at a wake? An old explanation: to **guard the body**, because the aswang came for the dead. In Hiligaynon-speaking Visayan lore, the amalanhig is a corpse that rises: a body so stiff it can't bend its knees or turn, so it can only go **in straight lines**, fast, smelling of rot. The advice repeated: run in zigzags, or climb.

**Claimed vs documented.**
- *Documented customs:* the wake (lamay) of three to seven nights, covered mirrors, no sweeping, pagpag. An Inquirer column traces the older lamay to guarding the corpse against the aswang.
- *Claimed:* the amalanhig. Its modern description partly traces to the *Pedro Penduko* komiks (per the Aswang Project), so we use only the core folk traits (stiff, straight lines, the smell, zigzag).

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Amalanhig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalanhig); [Aswang Project: Maranhig](https://aswangproject.com/maranhig); [Inquirer Opinion: 'Tao po! Hindi aswang!'](https://opinion.inquirer.net/?p=117125); [Wikipedia: Funeral practices in the Philippines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funeral_practices_and_burial_customs_in_the_Philippines).

**Scare beats.** The coffin's glass fogs from inside (hook) → 9 p.m.: the wake, card games, coffee, a covered mirror → 11 p.m.: the mourners thin; someone sweeps by mistake and is stopped → 1 a.m.: the last watcher dozes; a candle leans → 2 a.m.: the cloth over the mirror slides; in it, the coffin lid is open → 3 a.m.: the body is standing, rigid, arms at its sides → it moves in a dead straight line toward the sleeping watcher → the watcher wakes and runs, zigzagging between the monobloc chairs; it slams straight into the chairs, again and again → dawn: everyone is back; the body is in the coffin; its shoes are muddy.

**Form: "the clock."** One house, one night, in **hard cuts by the hour** (each opening on the same wall clock), with the camera always moving through the room in a slow orbit around the coffin, a little lower each hour, as if the room were sinking into sleep.
- **Scare placement:** the hook, then nothing but dread for half the episode, then one long chase (the only fast motion in the episode).
- **Sound language:** card-slaps, a pressure stove for coffee, rosary murmurs that thin out hour by hour, a gecko (tuko) calling; at 3 a.m. the gecko stops; the body's movement is a stiff wooden creak; the chase is chairs scraping.
- **Visual language:** fluorescent tube and candle light, the white of the coffin lining, a funeral parlour's lettering on a stand (invented name).

**Care.** No real family, town or parlour. The dead person is a stranger to the audience and never shown decayed or wounded. The narration explains the custom with respect: it is about love, not fear.

## Ep 36 "Sunugin Mo ang Damit" (Burn Your Clothes): the Ikot jeep

**The story.** UP Diliman's campus legend: late one night, a student rides the Ikot (the yellow-roofed jeep that circles the campus). One by one the passengers get off until she is alone. At her stop the driver tells her, quietly: when you get home, take off your clothes and **burn them**. Why? In the retellings, the driver could see what she couldn't: someone had been holding on to her all the way.

**Claimed vs documented.** A campus legend in Rappler's and Inquirer's school-story features; its endings vary, and I could not read a full version (the Inquirer page is blocked here). The Ikot itself is real: the route dates from 1949. Benitez Hall and other UP legends are also told.

**Sources (summaries):** [Rappler: Spooky school stories, UP Diliman edition](https://www.rappler.com/life-and-style/42725-spooky-school-stories-up-diliman-edition); [Inquirer Lifestyle: The spirits of UP Diliman](https://lifestyle.inquirer.net/?p=134129) (not reachable); [UP: the first Ikot driver's last ride](https://upd.edu.ph/1st-ikot-drivers-last-ride/).

**Scare beats.** The window's reflection shows a full bench; inside it's empty (hook) → the campus at night, acacias, the jeep's yellow roof → passengers get off stop by stop, each time looking back at her strangely → the fare coins on the bench: one more than there are passengers → she is alone; the reflection shows one figure beside her, its head bowed against her shoulder → the jeep stops; the driver won't look back; "Sunugin mo ang damit pagdating mo" → in her dorm she takes off her jacket: on its back, two grey handprints → she drops it in a can and burns it; in the smoke, a shape → the Ikot passes outside, empty, with the reflection of her sitting in it.

**Form: "two jeeps."** Every shot shows **the inside and its reflection at once** (the window, the polished steel, a puddle): the real jeep and the reflected jeep disagree. (Not Ep 11's rear-view mirror: here the driver is never in a mirror, and reflections are the world itself.)
- **Scare placement:** the hook, then a slow accumulation of wrongness; the reveal is the handprints (an object, not a face); a coda.
- **Sound language:** the jeep's diesel idle, the tap of a coin on the roof ("para!"), the bench's vinyl creaking under weight that isn't there, crickets, the fire.
- **Visual language:** the yellow roof and "IKOT" sign, acacia shadows, sodium lights; the reflected world slightly bluer and colder than the real one.

**Care.** A real university and real working drivers: no driver or student is named or shown as the source, the driver is kind (he saves her), and the legend is told as a legend. No real dorm named. No call to ride it at night.

## Ep 37 "Pitong Biyernes" (Seven Fridays): barang, Siquijor

**The story.** On Siquijor, folk belief holds that a *mambabarang* keeps insects (beetles, centipedes, bees) in a closed **bamboo tube**, and sends them, by name, to enter a victim's body; the victim falls sick from inside. The cure is a *mananambal*, a healer. Every Holy Week the island's healers gather herbs for seven Fridays of Lent and brew them on Black Saturday on Mount Bandilaan, while, as they say, "Christ is dead".

**Claimed vs documented.**
- *Documented:* the Holy Week healing gathering (news, a *Silliman Journal* paper, the tourism board); barang recorded by a Spanish chronicler in the 16th century; reports of curse-for-pay scams.
- *Claimed:* the barang working. One healer interviewed by the Inquirer dismissed barang altogether. Some say the craft has died out.

**Sources (summaries):** [Inquirer: The potions of Siquijor](https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/887715/the-potions-of-siquijor); [Rappler: regaining healing powers while Christ is dead](https://www.rappler.com/nation/166943-siquijor-regaining-healing-powers-while-christ-dead); [ABS-CBN: faith and mysticism mix in 'Healing Festival'](https://news.abs-cbn.com/life/04/15/22/in-siquijor-faith-and-mysticism-mix-during-holy-week); [Silliman Journal: The Holy Week Rites of the Mananambal](https://sillimanjournal.su.edu.ph/index.php/sj/article/view/226); [GMA: The sorcerer of Siquijor](https://gmanetwork.com/news/lifestyle/content/253853/the-sorcerer-of-siquijor/story); [Philstar 2025: debunking the myth](https://philstar.com/lifestyle/2025/02/14/2420177/debunking-manananggal-myth-siquijor-island-faith-healers).

**Scare beats.** A bamboo tube on a table; its cork lifts by itself (hook) → Good Friday: the island at dusk, church bells silent → a beetle crawls out and opens its wings → the insect's flight, low over the road, through a village, under a door → a sleeping man; it lands by his ear and goes in → morning: he can't stand; something moves under the skin of his arm (a ripple, not a wound) → Black Saturday: the healers' cauldron on the mountain, smoke → the healer puts a bottle to the man's mouth… and in the silence, from inside him, the tiny click of wings → he coughs: a beetle on the mat, upside down, legs moving.

**Form: "insect's-eye."** Most of the episode is shot **at insect scale** (macro lenses, the world huge): the inside of the tube, the grain of the table, the flight path at knee height, the giant sleeping face. Hard cuts to human scale only for the healer.
- **Scare placement:** the hook; a long flight sequence that is suspense, not a scare; the body horror (the ripple) in the middle; the final cough as the release-scare.
- **Sound language:** the scrape of chitin in bamboo (huge, close), wings, a church's silence on Good Friday, the sea, the cauldron boiling; no score.
- **Visual language:** macro depth-of-field (a soft background), a warm brown lamp world for the curse, the healers' fire and smoke on the mountain at golden hour.

**Care.** Siquijor's people have long suffered from the "witch island" label; the narration says most mananambal **heal**, that barang is belief, and that scams exist. No real healer, family or barangay is named or shown. No skin is broken; no gore. The Church is not mocked.

## Ep 38 "FWD: Ingat Kayo" (FWD: Be Careful): Maria Labo

**The story.** A Visayan urban legend of the 1990s and 2000s: a woman came home from working abroad and became an aswang; while her husband was away she cooked their children; he came home, understood, and hacked her face with a bolo (*labo*, "to hack", in Hiligaynon). She ran. Ever since, people say she's been seen: in Iloilo, in Capiz, in Negros, in Sorsogon… working as a manicurist, buying in the market, with a scar across her face.

**Claimed vs documented.** An urban legend with no documented crime behind it; the place and year change every time it's told. It spread by word of mouth and chain text messages. A 2015 film was based on it. A blog argues it may have started from a misremembered news story; nothing confirms that.

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Maria Labo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Labo); [Aswang Project: Maria Labo and Teniente Gimo](https://aswangproject.com/aswang-urban-legends); [GMA: the scarred woman who preyed on the children of Iloilo](https://www.gmanetwork.com/news/lifestyle/content/631208/maria-labo-the-scarred-woman-who-preyed-on-the-children-of-iloilo/story); [National Gallery Singapore: Maria Labo, aswang, female rage](https://www.nationalgallery.sg/magazine/maria-labo-aswang-female-rage).

**Scare beats.** A Nokia screen: "FWD: si Maria Labo nakabalik na…" (hook) → a town at night, someone forwards it → each new forward adds a detail, and the picture changes to match (she's in Iloilo; no, Capiz; no, here) → a manicure stall at the market by day, a woman with her hair over half her face → night, a mother pulls her kids inside and bars the door → a new text from an unknown number: "Asa kamo? Ara ko sa gwa." (Where are you? I'm outside.) → the phone's light shows the jalousie; through it, half a face, a scar → the phone buzzes in our hand: "FWD:".

**Form: "the chain."** The episode is **the rumour itself**: hard cuts between a 2000s phone screen (pixel font, signal bars, "1 message received") and what each message claims, so the "facts" visibly contradict each other.
- **Scare placement:** the hook, then mounting paranoia; the face only at the end; then the loop (the next forward) restarts the episode.
- **Sound language:** the Nokia message tone (synthesized, not the real ringtone), keypad clicks, a market, a bolo on a whetstone, then silence and a phone vibrating on wood.
- **Visual language:** the green-lit monochrome phone screen, warm market day, blue night; the scar always half hidden.

**Care.** **No child is harmed or shown dead; the crime is only a line of text, and we cut away.** The legend has been used against returning OFWs and women: the narration says it is a rumour with no victim on record, and does not blame OFWs. No real town is called "her" town. No real manicurist or market. No real number. No dares.

## Ep 39 "Sino ang Susundo" (Who Will Fetch You): the *sundo*

**The story.** A belief told in many Filipino families: when someone is about to die, a loved one who has already died **comes to fetch them**: a mother, a husband, a child. The dying person sees them, talks to them, asks them to wait. Families watching at the bedside see no one.

**Claimed vs documented.** A widespread folk belief (the 2009 film *Sundo* and the KMJS story used it). Hospice workers worldwide report "deathbed visions"; we may mention that as reported, not as proof. No specific case is used.

**Sources (summaries):** [GMA: 'Sinong susundo sa iyo?'](https://www.gmanetwork.com/entertainment/showbiznews/sinong-susundo-sa-iyo/5523/); [Wikipedia: Sundo (2009 film)](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Sundo_(2009_film)); [Aswang Project: Iloko beliefs regarding death](https://aswangproject.com/iloko-beliefs-practices-death); [Underscore: funerary superstitions](https://medium.com/underscore-online/folklores-footprints-the-funerary-superstitions-in-the-philippines-5026d8b130d2).

**Scare beats.** The watcher's monobloc chair; across the bed, a second chair, and someone sitting in it (hook) → night 1: an old man in a ward bed, his son watching; the old man smiles at the empty doorway → night 2: "Sabi ko, mamaya pa" (I said, later); the IV drips; the doorway is dark, but the drip's shadow on the wall is joined by a woman's → night 3: the son sleeps; the far chair has been moved closer → night 4: his father whispers, "Nandito na siya. Pero hindi ako ang sinusundo niya." (She's here. But it's not me she's fetching.) → the son turns: the woman in the doorway is his late mother, and she is looking **at him**.

**Form: "the nights."** Four nights, each a hard cut that opens on the same slow push across the ward toward the bed, a little closer each night, as if the camera is the visitor. Day never comes.
- **Scare placement:** a quiet hook; dread rising night by night; the twist (it's the son) at the end.
- **Sound language:** an oxygen concentrator, the drip, a far monitor beep, the ward's other patients coughing, slippers in the corridor, and a woman's voice humming a lullaby only in the gaps (wordless).
- **Visual language:** green-white fluorescent ward light, curtains on rails, the dark doorway that slowly fills.

**Care.** No real hospital; staff are kind. The father's death isn't shown. Grief is treated with tenderness; the "fetcher" is loving, which is what makes the twist frightening. Nothing claims the son will die; it ends on her look.

## Ep 40 "Patayin ang Ilaw" (Lights Off): Malinta Tunnel, Corregidor (finale)

**The story.** Malinta Tunnel on Corregidor was built in the 1920s–30s as a bombproof bunker; in 1942 its laterals became a 1,000-bed hospital for American and Filipino wounded under siege. In February 1945, Japanese troops trapped inside detonated explosives and died there; collapsed laterals have never been excavated. Today, on the island's night lateral tour, guides ask visitors to **switch off their flashlights**. Visitors report shadow figures, soldiers, cold spots, and the feeling of being watched.

**Claimed vs documented.**
- *Documented:* the tunnel's history (the hospital, the 1945 detonations; the dates differ between sources, 16–23 February); the night tour and the lights-off moment (travel accounts).
- *Claimed:* the apparitions (blogs and travel writers; no investigation).

**Sources (summaries):** [Wikipedia: Malinta Tunnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malinta_Tunnel); [WW2DB: Malinta Tunnel](https://ww2db.com/facility.php?q=94); [The Backpack Adventures: night lateral tour](https://www.thebackpackadventures.com/2015/11/malinta-tunnel-night-lateral-tour.html); [Coconuts Manila: Corregidor's overnight tour](https://coconuts.co/manila/news/corregidors-overnight-tour-ride-zipline-hike-hillsides-and-catch-some-ghosts); [SCMP: ghost of war haunts Corregidor](https://www.scmp.com/article/73516/ghost-war-haunts-corregidor).

**Scare beats.** Flashlights go out one by one in a lateral (hook) → the island at night, the tunnel mouth → the group walks the main tunnel; beams on concrete, old hospital-bed bolts in the floor → a lateral sealed by rubble; dust trickles down → "Patayin ang ilaw" → **eight seconds of black**, with sound only: breathing, a drip, then many more footsteps than there are of us, and a bed's metal frame creaking → lights on: the group is the same… one beam finds, at the back of the group, an extra figure in a 1940s helmet, head bowed → lights out by themselves → black → the tour guide counts heads, off-screen: one too many.

**Form: "the count."** A group POV that keeps **counting**: on-screen, the beams are the people (one beam = one visitor), and the number of beams changes. The centrepiece is the longest planned black in the channel's history, which must still frighten muted: the picture during the black is a faint after-image of the beams and, at its end, a dim glow where no one is standing.
- **Scare placement:** the hook; a long walk (history, suspense); the black as the main scare in the middle; a final count as the sting.
- **Sound language:** the island's cicadas cut dead at the tunnel mouth; concrete reverb; dripping; the guide's murmur (no words); in the black, footsteps multiplying and a far, old hospital bell; no score.
- **Visual language:** grey concrete, sodium-yellow beams, dust in the light; the extra figure only ever in the edge of one beam.

**Care.** Real dead of several nations: no side is villainised, no soldier's face, no wound, no gore, no depiction of the 1945 suicides (only the sealed rubble). A legitimate guided tour: no trespass, no "go after hours". The narration closes by honouring everyone who died there.
