## Folklore, science and their limits

Checked from web-search summaries only (The Aswang Project on santelmo and santilmo; Wikipedia-derived pages on the santelmo and on St. Elmo's fire).

| Claim in the video | Status |
|---|---|
| Farmers call it santelmo; a ball of fire over fields, rivers and the sea | Consistent with the belief as summarised (the name comes from St. Elmo's fire; older indigenous names exist) |
| St. Elmo's fire is real and known to science; it clings to ships' masts in storms | Consistent: a documented glow (a corona discharge on masts and points in stormy air) |
| It doesn't follow you home | The video's point: the real glow does not follow travellers |
| The old people say it is a soul, of someone who drowned and was never found | Consistent: a common belief (some say someone never properly mourned or buried) |
| It leads you to the water | Consistent: it leads travellers astray, into mud and water |
| Turn your clothes inside out | Consistent: a remedy reported in the folklore |
| The farmer, the field, the face | Fiction, built on the belief |

## Care

- Drowning is real in farming and fishing communities. No drowning is depicted; the face is a legend image, not a person, and no one is named.
- The science is stated plainly and kindly: St. Elmo's fire is real, and it doesn't follow you.
- No real place is named.
