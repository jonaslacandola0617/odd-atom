# Applying the expanded study to original artwork

This is an original-production guide derived from the case-study images in [the study](README.md). It does not assert that the full episodes use a particular animation technique or timing. Official reference images stay in the reference library; our scene geometry and characters should be authored separately.

## Match the visual treatment to the kind of question

| Kind of story | Observed reference approach | Proposed approach for our original work |
|---|---|---|
| A personal feeling | Separate people, inward poses, everyday props, purple tonal grouping | Stage a relatable situation; make posture and relationship readable before adding explanatory graphics |
| A question about trust | Familiar classroom; complexity concentrated on one board | Begin with a concrete example, then introduce an explicit visual metaphor |
| A large public danger | Extreme light/dark separation, city-scale comparison, damaged infrastructure | Establish scale and consequences; connect the event to recognizable systems |
| A shared problem | Repeated tiles and symbols; several characters around one object | Keep symbol meanings stable; show how parts relate rather than decorating a list of facts |
| A distant future | Foreground adult and child looking over a warm city | Anchor a large idea in a person, place, and meaningful choice |
| An unfamiliar scale | Stable traveler, large size differences, layered environments | Preserve a reference object while rebuilding the surrounding scene at each scale |

## A palette of settings

Our next original art explorations should include a street or station, an interior with practical objects, a workplace or classroom, a lived-in future city, and a diagram space for social systems. A space scene can remain one setting among these.

Build each setting from three levels: the large spatial masses, the objects that explain the story, and local details that support those objects. The reference classroom is a useful example: room and desks establish the setting; teacher, pupil, and board establish the relationship; the intricate branching pattern stays on the board. This gives detail a purpose.

In the future-city image, foreground people, a middle-distance domed structure, and a distant skyline remain distinct. In the infrastructure triptych, tilted rails and broken equipment occupy separate panels. These are different solutions to depth and density; one layout should not be reused for every subject.

## Color roles, not one permanent color recipe

| Visual mode | Observed large color relationships | What to test in original art |
|---|---|---|
| Social isolation | Deep purple environment; small pink and yellow accents | Reduce background contrast while keeping the face and gesture legible |
| Everyday differences | Shared purple field; cyan/yellow/pink local panels | Use local color to distinguish situations without making separate visual worlds |
| Explanation in a familiar room | Warm yellow setting; cool characters/windows; dark board | Concentrate contrast on the example being discussed |
| Immediate danger | White/yellow center, orange surroundings, dark purple city | Reserve the brightest values for the consequential event |
| Aftermath | Closely related purples with lighter equipment edges | Preserve object separation with value and overlap, not many unrelated colors |
| Collective systems | Cyan ground, cool repeated objects, red/magenta accents | Assign stable roles to categories and changes |
| A hopeful inhabited future | Warm skyline, dark foreground people, green planting | Separate viewpoint characters from the environment while keeping the setting welcoming |
| Exploration | Green/teal or cyan worlds, warmer/magenta focal subjects | Keep the traveler legible as the surrounding palette changes |

These emotional labels are interpretations for production, not claims of universal color psychology or the studio's documented intent.

## A human cast that can act

The Loneliness images contain varied hair masses, clothing, proportions, and poses. The future image also establishes a relationship between two people through overlap and a shared viewpoint. For our own characters, sketch distinct silhouettes and meaningful resting poses before building a rig.

Separate the torso, head/hair masses, upper/lower arms, hands, pelvis, and legs where articulation needs them. Test silhouettes for leaning, sitting, turning, reaching, carrying an object, and looking toward another character. Keep arm and hand contacts consistent with props. A character should be able to explain an action in a still pose before motion is added.

This is a proposed rigging workflow, not a reverse-engineered description of the source rigs. No supplied retired character asset needs to be restored.

## Let props explain the mechanism

The classroom's board, the climate case's repeated tiles and central prop, the city's scale label, and the future scene's architecture each perform a different explanatory job. Choose the prop after identifying the question it must answer.

For an original social-system scene, a queue might explain unequal access, a shared valve might explain a bottleneck, and a network might explain the spread of information. These are proposed metaphors, not claims that the studied episodes contain these particular scenes. Check that the metaphor's rules remain consistent and do not hide the explanation's limitations.

## Text and titles: what this evidence supports

The visible **2 km** annotation answers a concrete scale question. It supports using a plain, readable diagram label when the number matters. Most selected images carry their meaning through objects and staging without large explanatory headings.

This set does not contain enough main titles or subtitles to establish their construction. The user's existing requirements remain in force: layered outline passes for the appropriate opening title; short white narration captions with a thin dark edge; no added branding, badges, timers, schematic notes, or decorative production text. These are project requirements, not new findings from this study.

## Motion and sound: proposed tests, not observed findings

Once full episodes become accessible, inspect the relation between spoken ideas and poses, holds, camera movement, object transformations, transitions, score, and effects. Still images cannot answer those questions.

For original prototypes, a useful next test would be a human scene with a change of attention, an object with an understandable cause and effect, and a transition from a personal situation to a larger system. Lock the narration first, then author poses and timing against it. Evaluate whether motion improves the explanation rather than adding motion to every layer.

The pending ElevenLabs test is separate from this research; no speech generation was performed as part of the study.
